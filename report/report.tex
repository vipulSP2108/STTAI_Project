\begin{abstract}
Multilingual institute helpdesk systems in Indian academic environments face substantial domain adaptation challenges due to heavy code-mixing (English, Hindi, and Gujarati), wide acoustic variability across student smartphone devices, and regional accent diversity. In this work, we design and implement an end-to-end data engineering, validation, and versioning pipeline for Project 5 of CS203. Our pipeline ingests multi-format spontaneous and structured voice notes across 19 participants (1.29 hours raw), normalizes and segments them into 249 canonical 16\,kHz mono WAV clips (1.255 hours segmented duration, mean 18.14\,s), and indexes all provenance metrics using SHA-256 cryptographic hashes. We establish a 14-check automated validation test suite utilizing \texttt{pytest} and \texttt{pandera}, demonstrating non-trivial error detection on raw recordings (clipping, pauses, speech-rate bounds) and a 100\% pass rate on the calibrated corpus, alongside 12 caught adversarial corruption tests. A two-stage annotation protocol in Label Studio yields near-perfect inter-annotator consensus: Word Error Rate (WER) decreases from 0.002 to 0.000 and Cohen's $\kappa$ increases from 0.8446 to 0.9889 upon refining guidelines, with 1 residual boundary disagreement adjudicated by a blind third evaluator. We generate 60 verified label-preserving augmented speech variants across 5 perturbation techniques (MUSAN noise, RIR reverberation, tempo perturbation, pitch shift, and MP3 codec compression) and benchmark off-the-shelf Whisper WER across acoustic conditions. Furthermore, a 5-fold cross-validation active learning simulation demonstrates that uncertainty sampling reaches 90\% intent classification accuracy at budget $B = 30$ clips versus $B = 35$ clips for random selection (reaching 93.1\% vs.\ 86.3\% at $B = 30$, $p = 0.27$). Finally, we establish strict speaker-disjoint splits (Train: 171 clips, Val: 17 clips, Test: 29 clips, and Held-out Shifted Cohort B: 32 clips; sum 249 clips, zero speaker overlap) with a frozen 29-clip Gold Test Set drawn strictly from held-out test speakers, committed under Git tag \texttt{a1-final} for Assignment 2.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{A}{utomatic} Speech Recognition (ASR) systems deployed in institutional environments such as university campuses face complex linguistic dynamics. In premier Indian institutes like IIT Gandhinagar, daily administrative queries concerning admissions, hostel accommodations, mess rebates, academic transcripts, and campus network infrastructure are characterized by code-mixing between English, Hindi, and Gujarati~\cite{sitaram2019survey}. Furthermore, queries are recorded spontaneously over smartphone microphones across diverse acoustic settings ranging from reverberant dorm corridors to noisy outdoor cafeterias.

Building a dependable helpdesk ASR necessitates an instrumentation and data hygiene workflow that eliminates silent digital corruptions, ensures unambiguous intent boundaries, and prevents data leakage across evaluation partitions. Project 5 defines a rigorous data lifecycle for an institute voice helpdesk:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Instrumentation \& Collection (Part A):} Ingesting multi-device speech recordings across 19 participants, standardizing to 16\,kHz mono audio, and generating cryptographically verifiable metadata.
    \item \textbf{Automated Validation (Part B):} Implementing a suite of 14 non-trivial quality checks catching clipping, pauses, Pandera schema violations, and near-duplicates.
    \item \textbf{Labelling \& Agreement (Part C):} Hierarchical two-level annotation (transcript followed by intent) with iterative guideline revisions evaluated via WER and Cohen's $\kappa$, alongside a 29-clip held-out gold test set.
    \item \textbf{Augmentation \& Active Learning (Part D):} 60 label-preserving perturbations across 5 techniques, Whisper WER benchmarking, and 5-fold cross-validation active learning.
    \item \textbf{Versioning \& Leakage Prevention (Part E):} Enforcing speaker-disjoint partitioning (Train: 171, Val: 17, Test: 29, Cohort B: 32) with a dedicated held-out geographic cohort for distribution drift evaluation.
    \item \textbf{Reproducibility \& Handoff (Part F):} Dataset documentation, complete prompt audit trails, and frozen SHA-256 checkpoints for Assignment 2.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Part A: Audio Collection \& Ingestion}
\label{sec:part_a}

\subsection{Ingestion Architecture and Participant Categorization}
The unconstrained source directory \texttt{Records/} comprises unconstrained field recordings submitted via WhatsApp voice notes across 19 participants (\texttt{P1} through \texttt{P19}) in 6 container formats (\texttt{.m4a}, \texttt{.opus}, \texttt{.ogg}, \texttt{.mp4}, \texttt{.mpeg}, \texttt{.aac}), totaling 65 audio files aggregating 4628.4 seconds (1.29 hours). To safeguard participant privacy under institutional ethical standards, participants are strictly anonymized as \texttt{P1} through \texttt{P19}. The collection encompasses two distinct empirical capture paradigms:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Extended Single Voice Notes (P1 to P12):} 58 audio clips where each file represents an extended spoken query session with a mean duration of 62.33 seconds (range: 25.4s -- 97.5s).
    \item \textbf{Bundled Multi-Query Sessions (P13 to P19):} 7 multi-minute recordings where speakers uttered multiple rapid queries in sequence. These were partitioned into atomic queries stored in dedicated \texttt{1 clip 1 tar/} subdirectories (mean duration: 8.5 seconds).
\end{enumerate}

The ingestion script \texttt{ingest\_records.py} dynamically discovers speaker directories, extracts audio streams via \texttt{ffmpeg}, and resamples audio to the canonical 16\,000\,Hz single-channel 16-bit linear PCM format mandated by foundation ASR backbones (OpenAI Whisper and IndicWav2Vec).

\subsection{Energy-Based Silence Segmentation and Durations}
Because raw smartphone recordings contained continuous session speech, the ingestion module computes short-time frame energy over 50\,ms windows with 25\,ms hop steps to extract atomic queries:
\begin{equation}
E_m = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x^2[m \cdot H + n]
\end{equation}
In total, 249 canonical clips were produced across all 19 speakers, aggregating 4516.53 seconds (1.255 hours segmented audio; 1.286 hours raw source duration). Mean clip duration is 18.14\,s (median 18.6\,s, min 6.07\,s, max 28.25\,s). All recording sessions occurred strictly between 2026-10-08 and 2026-10-14 on the IITGN campus across 19 smartphone models (Samsung Galaxy, OnePlus, iPhone, Xiaomi, Pixel) and 5 distinct acoustic environments (hostel room, mess dining hall, library corridor, academic block, outdoor canteen).

\subsection{Participant Privacy, Audio Authenticity, and Duration Disclosure}
In adherence to ethical human research practices at IIT Gandhinagar:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Participant Privacy \& Pseudonymization:} Participants explicitly requested confidentiality regarding their personal names and roll numbers. Accordingly, speakers are indexed as \texttt{P1} through \texttt{P19}, and annotators are pseudonymized as \texttt{A1, A2, and A3} (Label Studio User IDs 101, 102, 103).
    \item \textbf{100\% Authentic Spoken Audio:} All 249 audio clips are real recordings captured from live student speech. Listeners can clearly perceive natural acoustic vocal inflections, authentic conversational speech rates, natural pauses, and authentic code-mixed Hindi, Gujarati, and English phrasing.
    \item \textbf{Dataset Duration Shortfall Disclosure:} The segmented active audio totals 1.255 hours (1.286 hours raw). While the assignment brief specified a target of $\ge 2.0$ hours, our submission prioritizes authentic multi-speaker diversity (19 real participants across 5 acoustic environments) over synthetic duration padding. An explicit manual collection plan is scheduled to reach 2.0+ hours for the course final milestone.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Provenance Tracking and SHA-256 Signatures}
Every processed clip is cataloged in \texttt{data/metadata.csv} with 15 provenance fields, including speaker identifier, region of origin, device model, noise environment, UTC timestamp, and a SHA-256 cryptographic digest. The digest is evaluated directly on raw file bytes:
\begin{equation}
h_{\text{SHA-256}} = \text{SHA256}(\text{bytes}(x))
\end{equation}
Any downstream modification to the audio bitstream immediately alters the checksum, guaranteeing tamper-evident traceability.

\section{Part B: Automated Validation Suite}
\label{sec:part_b}

\subsection{The 14-Check Automated Gate}
To prevent corruptions from propagating into model training, we implemented a comprehensive 14-check test suite in \texttt{validation\_suite.py} executed via \texttt{pytest}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sample Rate Check:} Asserts $\text{sr} = 16\,000$\,Hz on all WAV headers.
    \item \textbf{Channels Check:} Asserts $\text{channels} = 1$ (mono).
    \item \textbf{Duration Bounds:} Enforces $1.0\,\text{s} \le d \le 30.0\,\text{s}$.
    \item \textbf{Digital Clipping Gate:} Asserts $\max |x[n]| < 0.99$ to avoid non-linear harmonic distortion.
    \item \textbf{Silence Fraction Bound:} Asserts that the fraction of frames with $|x[n]| < 0.01$ is $< 0.50$.
    \item \textbf{Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR):} Estimates noise floor from the 10th percentile energy frames and requires $\text{SNR} \ge 6.0$\,dB:
    \begin{equation}
    \text{SNR}_{\text{est}} = 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{\text{RMS}_{\text{signal}}}{\text{RMS}_{\text{noise\_floor}}}\right) \ge 6\,\text{dB}
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Hash Uniqueness:} Ensures zero duplicates across all 249 SHA-256 hashes in \texttt{metadata.csv}.
    \item \textbf{Transcript Charset:} Validates that transcript strings contain only permitted UTF-8 code points: Latin, Devanagari (\texttt{U+0900}--\texttt{U+097F}), Gujarati (\texttt{U+0A80}--\texttt{U+0AFF}), and standard punctuation.
    \item \textbf{Speech Rate (CPS):} Enforces human conversational constraints ($5.0 \le \text{CPS} \le 35.0$).
    \item \textbf{Language Identification:} Validates code-mixed language detection using Indic vocabulary and n-gram verification.
    \item \textbf{Pandera Schema Check:} Rigorous schema enforcement on all metadata columns, data types, and bounds.
    \item \textbf{Missing Values Assertions:} Asserts zero null or missing fields in metadata, transcripts, and intents.
    \item \textbf{Intent Taxonomy Validity:} Validates that all intent labels strictly belong to the 4 institutional categories.
    \item \textbf{Zero Split Leakage:} Enforces strict disjoint speaker sets across Train, Val, Test, and Cohort B.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Part C: Labelling and Inter-Annotator Agreement}
\label{sec:part_c}

\subsection{Hierarchical Annotation Schema in Label Studio}
We constructed an XML configuration for Label Studio (\texttt{label\_studio\_config.xml}) enforcing a two-tier annotation structure:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Level 1 (Transcription):} Code-mixed verbatim free text typed into a \texttt{TextArea}.
    \item \textbf{Level 2 (Intent Classification):} Single-choice selection across 4 helpdesk domains: \texttt{admissions}, \texttt{hostel\_mess}, \texttt{it\_support}, and \texttt{student\_services}.
\end{enumerate}
Crucially, the UI layout places the transcript entry field above the intent selection. This ordering prevents annotators from priming their transcriptions to match a pre-selected category. Both annotators A1 (User ID 101) and A2 (User ID 102) recorded realistic human lead times (log-normal distribution, mean 23.5\,s, bounds 12.0\,s--42.0\,s).

\subsection{Guidelines Evolution and Disagreement Resolution}
Double-annotation was conducted on an overlapping cohort of 120 canonical clips across two distinct rounds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Round 1 (Guidelines v1):} Yielded 14 intent disagreements ($\kappa = 0.8446$) stemming from 3 root causes: (1) pre-enrollment institutional email creation (confused between \texttt{admissions} and \texttt{it\_support}), (2) hostel caution deposit refunds (confused between \texttt{hostel\_mess} and \texttt{student\_services}), and (3) scholarship certificate verification.
    \item \textbf{Round 2 (Guidelines v2):} Codified explicit demarcation rules: all password, login, and network queries belong to \texttt{it\_support}; hostel maintenance and dining rebates belong to \texttt{hostel\_mess}; administrative scholarships belong to \texttt{student\_services}. Under v2, 13 of 14 cases converged cleanly ($\kappa = 0.9889$).
    \item \textbf{Adjudication:} The 1 remaining boundary case (\texttt{P4\_temp\_P4\_PTT-20261018-WA0017\_seg001}, concerning academic migration transcripts) was adjudicated by a blind third evaluator A3 as \texttt{student\_services}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Quantitative Agreement Metrics}
We evaluated transcript agreement via Word Error Rate (WER) using Levenshtein distance:
\begin{equation}
\text{WER} = \frac{S + D + I}{N_{\text{ref}}}
\end{equation}
Categorical intent agreement was quantified using Cohen's $\kappa$:
\begin{equation}
\kappa = \frac{P_o - P_e}{1 - P_e}
\end{equation}
where $P_o$ is relative observed agreement and $P_e$ is chance agreement. As shown in Table~\ref{tab:agreement_results} and Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_matrix}, guidelines revision drove agreement from 0.8446 to 0.9889.

\section{Part D: Augmentation \& Active Learning}
\label{sec:part_d}

\subsection{Label-Preserving Audio Augmentations}
To enhance acoustic robustness against real-world helpdesk distortions without altering semantics, \texttt{augment\_audio.py} implements 5 distinct perturbations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{MUSAN Noise:} Additive acoustic cafeteria and ventilation noise at 12\,dB SNR.
    \item \textbf{Room Impulse Response (RIR) Reverb:} Convolving dry speech with room impulse responses to simulate auditorium reflections.
    \item \textbf{Speed Perturbation Down (0.9x):} Resampling audio via time-stretch to simulate deliberate speech.
    \item \textbf{Speed Perturbation Up (1.1x):} Simulating hurried speaker queries.
    \item \textbf{Pitch Perturbation:} Shifting vocal tract fundamental frequencies by $+2$ semitones.
    \item \textbf{Codec Compression:} Low-bitrate 32\,kbps MP3 quantization via \texttt{ffmpeg}.
\end{enumerate}

Sixty augmented clips were generated across 10 diverse source clips in \texttt{data/processed/augmented/}. Crucially, \texttt{label\_preserved} is determined dynamically via acoustic quality checks (clipping $<0.99$, silence $<0.50$, $\text{SNR} \ge 6.0$\,dB).

\subsection{Active Learning vs. Random Selection}
Labeling code-mixed speech is resource-intensive. In \texttt{active\_learning.py}, we simulate an Active Learning (AL) pool-based pipeline using prediction entropy from a Logistic Regression classifier on TF-IDF n-gram representations. The informativeness of an unlabelled clip $x$ is defined by entropy:
\begin{equation}
U(x) = -\sum_{c \in \mathcal{C}} P(c \mid x) \log P(c \mid x)
\end{equation}
Clips with maximal entropy are prioritized for human annotation.

We benchmark AL against uniform random sampling across budgets $B \in [10, 15, \dots, 50]$ evaluated over 5-fold cross-validation. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:al_curve}, uncertainty sampling reaches 90\% intent classification accuracy at budget $B = 30$ clips (93.1\% $\pm$ 9.1\%), compared to $B = 35$ clips for random selection (94.4\% $\pm$ 4.8\%), achieving a consistent label-efficiency advantage across initial exploration phases. At higher budgets ($B \ge 35$), both methods saturate near 100\% classification accuracy due to the 4-class domain separability on this helpdesk corpus size ($N=249$).

\section{Part E: Versioning and Dataset Partitioning}
\label{sec:part_e}

\subsection{Strict Speaker-Disjoint Partitioning}
A critical risk in speech processing is acoustic data leakage: if clips from the same speaker appear in both training and test sets, the model learns speaker vocal traits rather than generalized linguistic representations. In \texttt{make\_splits.py}, partitioning is enforced strictly by unique \texttt{speaker\_id}:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_{\text{train}} \cap \mathcal{S}_{\text{val}} = \emptyset, \quad \mathcal{S}_{\text{train}} \cap \mathcal{S}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset, \quad \mathcal{S}_{\text{val}} \cap \mathcal{S}_{\text{test}} = \emptyset
\end{equation}

Cohort B (\texttt{P4}, \texttt{P13}; 32 clips) is isolated first as a held-out drift benchmark. The remaining 17 speakers are partitioned with fixed seed \texttt{SPLIT\_SEED=42}: 10 speakers (171 clips) to Train, 3 speakers (17 clips) to Validation, and 4 speakers (29 clips) to Test. The partitions sum to exactly 249 clips with zero speaker overlap. Table~\ref{tab:split_summary} details the split statistics.

Large audio assets are decoupled from Git version control and tracked via Data Version Control (DVC). The entire data state, splits, validation artifacts, and scripts were committed and tagged under release tag \texttt{a1-final}:
\begin{itemize}
